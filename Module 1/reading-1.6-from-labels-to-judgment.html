Description before evaluation

The first five lessons developed tools for thinking clearly: distinguish automation from learning, name tasks precisely, examine inputs and outputs, recognize history and hype, and separate behavior from claims about understanding. The final step is judgment.

Good judgment does not mean refusing to make claims. It means making claims proportional to evidence. A useful AI Field Guide helps a reader see what a system can do and where confidence should stop.

Weak descriptionStronger, evidence-based description
“The app understands students and knows the best way for them to learn.”“The app asks students to answer practice questions, records answer patterns, and recommends additional exercises. The available evidence suggests it ranks activities using those patterns; this does not show that it knows a student’s full needs or circumstances.”

Four habits of responsible AI description

  1. Name the task. Say whether the system classifies, predicts, recommends, generates, perceives, or plans.
  2. Name the evidence. Identify whether the claim comes from direct observation, documentation, credible reporting, or an inference.
  3. Name uncertainty. Use phrases such as appears to, likely, the company states, or the available evidence suggests when you cannot verify a claim directly.
  4. Name limitations and human roles. Explain a failure mode, risk, or boundary and identify a human decision involved in the system.

Judgment is not cynicism

Responsible skepticism is not dismissing every technology. A system may be genuinely useful for a bounded task. Clear limits make good use more likely. A navigation app can save time even though it does not understand your life. A language model can help generate questions even though it may invent sources. A classifier can help sort images even though it may fail on unfamiliar cases.

The practical question is: What is this system good for, under what conditions, with what oversight, and with what limits?

Evidence, inference, and unsupported assertion

  • Evidence-based claim: supported by observation, documentation, data, or credible sources.
  • Inference: a conclusion drawn from available evidence rather than directly observed fact.
  • Unsupported assertion: a statement presented as fact without enough evidence.
Final Field Guide test: Could a reader tell what the system does, how you know, what remains uncertain, who is responsible, and when the system may be a poor fit?

Key vocabulary

  • anthropomorphism: describing a nonhuman system as if it had human feelings, intentions, or understanding.
  • evidence-based claim: a statement supported by observation, documentation, data, or credible sources.
  • inference: a conclusion drawn from available evidence rather than directly observed fact.
  • limitation: a condition under which a system may fail, be unreliable, or be inappropriate.
  • human oversight: a person’s responsibility to monitor, evaluate, approve, or correct a system’s output or action.
