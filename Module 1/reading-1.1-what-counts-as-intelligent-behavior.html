Intelligence is not one simple thing

In ordinary speech, we often call a person intelligent when that person can do more than repeat a memorized response. Intelligence may involve noticing what matters, remembering information, representing a situation, comparing possibilities, learning from feedback, explaining reasons, communicating, planning, and changing course when circumstances change.

No single list settles the meaning of intelligence. A gifted musician, a careful mechanic, a skilled nurse, and a thoughtful historian may all display intelligence in different ways. Their abilities overlap, but they are not identical. The same caution applies when we describe machines. A system that recognizes objects in a photograph, predicts the next word in a sentence, recommends a song, or finds a route through traffic may display an important capability. It does not automatically display every capability associated with human intelligence.

A habit for this course

When someone calls a system “intelligent,” separate three questions:

  1. What can the system do?
  2. How does it produce that result?
  3. What, if anything, does the result show about understanding?

From inputs to outputs

One practical way to study a system is to ask what information it receives and what it produces. An input is information a system receives: a word, a photograph, a location, a sensor reading, a click, or a number. An output is what the system produces: a label, a prediction, a recommendation, a sentence, a route, a score, or an action.

Inputs and outputs alone are not enough. We also need to ask what process connects them. A system may follow a fixed rule, search among possible options, compare a new case to patterns in previous data, or combine several methods. It is also useful to ask whether the system changes over time. Some systems never change unless a person changes their instructions. Others are updated, retrained, or designed to respond to new information.

Automation, adaptation, and judgment

Automation means that a system carries out a process with little ongoing human intervention. Automation can be simple: a sprinkler turns on every Tuesday at 6:00 a.m. It can also be complicated: a warehouse system may coordinate thousands of items. But an automated system is not necessarily a learning system.

Adaptation means that a system’s behavior changes in response to feedback, data, or changed conditions. A route planner that reacts to traffic may adapt to current conditions. A machine-learning system may be retrained on new examples. Adaptation can make a system more useful, but it does not guarantee that the system is correct, fair, or wise.

Human judgment includes more than choosing an output. People can ask whether a goal is worth pursuing, consider duties and consequences, account for context that was not supplied in advance, and accept responsibility for a decision. Human beings can also make mistakes. The point is not that every human decision is perfect; it is that responsibility and moral judgment are not automatically transferred to a tool because the tool makes a recommendation.

Why careful language matters

Calling a system “intelligent” can be helpful when it directs attention to real abilities such as recognizing patterns, planning, using language, or adapting to feedback. The same label can mislead when it makes us assume a system has human-like understanding, motives, wisdom, or moral responsibility.

Compare these claims:

  • Vague: “The app understands me.”
  • More careful: “The app uses my past clicks and the behavior of similar users to predict what I may select next.”

The second statement is more useful because it describes a possible process, makes a claim that can be investigated, and leaves room to ask whether the prediction is accurate, fair, or appropriate.

      Key vocabulary

      • automationA system carrying out a process with little ongoing human intervention.
      • algorithmA defined set of steps used to solve a problem or complete a task.
      • inputInformation a system receives.
      • outputInformation or an action a system produces.
      • adaptationA change in behavior based on feedback, data, or changed conditions.
      • intelligent behaviorBehavior that appears to involve abilities such as perceiving, learning, reasoning, planning, communicating, or adapting; the phrase does not settle whether a system understands.
