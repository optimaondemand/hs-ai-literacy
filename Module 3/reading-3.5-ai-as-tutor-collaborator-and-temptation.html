AI Literacy · Module 3 · Reading 3.5

AI as Tutor, Collaborator, and Temptation

Estimated time: 25–35 minutesRead independently

Essential question: When does AI support learning, and when does it quietly replace the work you need to do?
By the end: Apply academic-integrity principles to individual AI-use scenarios.

Begin with a prediction

Before reading, write two sentences: What would persuade you that an AI-generated statement is trustworthy? What would make you hesitate?

Core idea

Generative AI produces new language, images, audio, or code by modeling patterns in large collections of examples. Its output can be useful, surprising, and fluent. Fluency, however, is not a guarantee of accuracy, evidence, authorship, or understanding. Responsible work begins by separating what a system produces from what you can justify.

How to reason carefully

1. Name the task

Is the system brainstorming, summarizing, drafting, retrieving, classifying, or making a claim? Different tasks require different checks.

2. Inspect the evidence

Ask where a factual claim came from. A citation is a lead, not proof, until you open the source and check whether it supports the exact statement.

3. Retain judgment

Decide what to accept, revise, narrow, or reject. You remain responsible for claims published under your name.

Worked example

Suppose a system writes: “The policy reduced absenteeism by 40 percent.” A careful researcher does not repeat the sentence because it sounds precise. The researcher asks: Which policy? In what place and time period? What study measured the result? Does the source actually report 40 percent, and what were its limits?

Common misconception

Misconception: “If the system gives a citation, the claim is verified.”
Correction: Citations can be incomplete, irrelevant, fabricated, or misread. Verification requires opening and evaluating the source.

Key vocabulary

TermStudent-friendly meaning
Generative modelA model that produces new content based on learned patterns.
GroundingConnecting an answer to reliable, inspectable sources or provided evidence.
HallucinationA confident-sounding output that is unsupported, false, or invented.
VerificationChecking a claim against credible evidence, not merely trusting its presentation.
AuthorshipResponsible ownership of the choices, claims, evidence, and final form of work.
