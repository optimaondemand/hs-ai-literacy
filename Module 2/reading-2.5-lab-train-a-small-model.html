Lab: Train a Small Model

Read before beginning the model lab

Estimated time
15 minutes
Focus question
What evidence should you record when a small model learns, succeeds, or fails?
Required for
Lesson 2.5: Training Data Log and First-Round Tests

Begin with a prediction. If you train a classifier only on neat, centered examples, what might happen when it sees a tilted, distant, or cluttered example?

The goal is investigation, not a scoreboard

In this lab, you may use an approved no-code image, sound, or text classifier. If a live tool is unavailable, you will use a teacher-provided simulated training run. Both paths meet the same learning goals: document categories, training examples, tests, errors, suspected causes, and limits.

Prepare categories and examples

Use a small, bounded task with safe material. Examples might include sorting teacher-provided image cards, classifying short fictional messages, or separating simple self-created shape drawings. Avoid real people, private records, sensitive attributes, or high-stakes judgments.

Each category should have multiple examples and meaningful variation. If all examples share the same background, angle, wording, or format, the model may learn a shortcut rather than the intended feature.

Record your data decisions

RecordWhy it matters
Category name and definitionShows what the model is supposed to distinguish.
Number/type of training examplesShows the evidence available for learning.

Test case and expected resultMakes evaluation inspectable.
Actual result/errorShows what happened, not what you hoped.

Test with unfamiliar examples

Do not count a training example as proof of performance. Use different examples for testing. Include normal cases, ambiguous cases, and cases that vary background, wording, scale, noise, or formatting. Record the result even if it is disappointing.

Lab integrity: A model that fails honestly is more valuable for learning than a polished claim with missing records. Do not alter records to make results look better.

Troubleshooting questions

  • Are categories defined clearly enough to label examples consistently?
  • Do training examples contain enough variation?
  • Is the test example too similar to a training example?
  • Did a background, format, or accidental cue become a shortcut?
  • Does the task remain appropriate and low-risk?

As you move to the lesson

Bring your experiment plan. Be ready to log every training-data decision and at least 12 first-round tests.
