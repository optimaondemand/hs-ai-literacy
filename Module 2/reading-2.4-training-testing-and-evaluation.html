Training, Testing, and Evaluation

Reading before the lesson activity

Estimated time
15–20 minutes
Focus question
How can testing reveal both a model’s strengths and the kinds of errors it makes?
Required for
Lesson 2.4: Model Experiment Plan

Begin with a prediction. A classifier gets 90 out of 100 examples right. Is that enough to call it safe or useful? What else would you need to know?

Train on some examples; test on different examples

Training adjusts a model using examples. Testing checks how the adjusted model performs on examples that were not used to train it. If a model is tested only on its training examples, high performance may show memorization rather than useful generalization.

A train/test split separates examples for learning from examples for evaluation. A model can still fail in real use if its test set is narrow, too similar to training data, or missing important conditions.

Overfitting and generalization

Overfitting occurs when a model becomes too tailored to patterns or noise in training data and performs poorly on new examples. Generalization means performing reasonably on relevant new examples. Neither word promises perfect performance; both direct attention to what evidence the test set provides.

Confusion matrix: inspect error types

For a two-category classifier, a confusion matrix counts four outcomes:

Actually positiveActually negative
Predicted positiveTrue positive (TP)False positive (FP)
Predicted negativeFalse negative (FN)True negative (TN)

Accuracy is the share of all predictions that are correct. It can hide important patterns. If rare but serious cases are missed, accuracy may look high while false negatives remain costly.

Precision asks: of items predicted positive, how many actually were positive? Recall asks: of items that actually were positive, how many did the model find? Different contexts may value these differently. A medical screening example, a spam filter, and a school-resource triage tool each have different consequences for false positives and false negatives.

Confidence is not a guarantee

Some models produce confidence-like scores. A high score is not proof of truth. It may reflect patterns in the model’s representation, not knowledge of all relevant facts. Evaluation should consider examples, error types, conditions, and consequences—not a score alone.

Evaluation habit: Before celebrating a percentage, ask: What was tested? What was not tested? Which errors occurred? Who bears the cost of each error? What change would count as improvement?

Key vocabulary

  • train/test split: separation of examples used for learning and examples used for evaluation.
  • overfitting: fitting training patterns too closely and performing poorly on new cases.
  • generalization: useful performance on relevant new cases.
  • precision: share of predicted positives that are actually positive.
  • recall: share of actual positives that are found.
