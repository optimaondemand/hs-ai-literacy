Today’s Focus Question: How do observations become data representations that support, but do not replace, judgment?

What you will be able to do

  • Distinguish observations, labels, categories, claims, and decisions.
  • Explain why data are representations rather than complete reality.
  • Identify features and missing context in a familiar process.

Materials: Reading 2.1, Canvas text-entry access, and one familiar app or school process. No external account is required.

Lesson sequence

  1. 1

    Retrieve and predict

    5 minutes

    Classify these: “72°F,” “hot day,” “the classroom is uncomfortable,” and “open the window.” Which are an observation, category/label, claim, and decision?

    Interactive · Warm-up sortFour phrases, four bins

    Drag each phrase into a bin, or click a phrase and then a bin. Press Check when all four are placed. The bin definitions come straight from the reading.

    Observation

    A recorded fact or measurement.

    Category / label

    A tag attached to an observation, or a group defined by a rule or convention.

    Claim

    A statement that interprets evidence.

    Decision

    An action selected using information and judgment.

    0 of 4 placed
    Four phrases. Which one is a measurement, which is a tag, which interprets, and which acts?
  2. 2

    Experience: data-type sorter

    8 minutes

    For each item, mark observation, label, category, claim, decision, or more than one: a photo; “cat” written on a photo; a school’s “on time” rule; a statement that a student is disengaged; a recommendation to contact a family; a count of missed assignments.

    Interactive · The Data-Type SorterType, justify, then debrief

    Each item is a row of the six-row table. Press one or more type chips (two or more means “more than one”). Then pick the reading’s definition that justifies your type, and pick what the record most likely leaves out. When all six are typed, press Debrief. More than one type can be reasonable; the debrief shows which, as long as your definition backs it.

    0 of 6 typed0 of 6 justified0 of 6 missing-context picks
    Six items. Give each a type, a definition, and a missing-context pick.
    ItemYour type · definition · missingBest supported by the reading

    Accessible text fallback: Make a six-row table with columns item, type, why, what may be missing. There may be more than one reasonable answer if you explain it.

    Feedback check: A label is not the same as a fact about a person. “Disengaged” is an interpretation that may need more context. A decision is an action, not merely a prediction.
